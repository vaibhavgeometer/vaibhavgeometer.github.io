The Mathematical Theory of Solved (m,n,k)-Games

1. Nash Strategy-Stealing Theorem

In no $(m,n,k)$-game can the second player (Player O) ever possess a forced winning strategy. An extra stone placed anywhere on the board can never disadvantage the player who owns it. Therefore, if the second player had a hypothetical winning strategy, the first player could place an arbitrary move on ply 1 and "steal" the strategy. Hence, every game is either a First-Player Win ($W$) or a Draw ($D$).

2. Sub-Board & Super-Board Monotonicity

$(m,n,k)$-games satisfy strict spatial monotonicity:
• If $(m_0, n_0, k)$ is a proven Draw, then any smaller sub-board $(m, n, k)$ with $m \le m_0$ and $n \le n_0$ is guaranteed to be a Draw.
• If $(m_0, n_0, k)$ is a proven Win, then any larger super-board $(m, n, k)$ with $m \ge m_0$ and $n \ge n_0$ is guaranteed to be a Win.

3. Pairing Strategies & Ramsey Theory

A pairing strategy partitions the cells of the board into disjoint pairs such that every winning line of length $k$ contains both cells of at least one pair. Whenever Player X moves in one cell of a pair, Player O responds in the other. Hales & Jewett (1963) and subsequent researchers proved that for $k \ge 8$, a pairing strategy forces a draw on all board sizes up to $(\infty, \infty, k)$.

Breakthrough Timeline in (m,n,k) Game Theory

  • k = 1 & k = 2: Trivial 1-ply wins for all boards except $(1,1,2)$ and $(1,2,2)$ which are draws.
  • k = 3: Classical Tic-Tac-Toe $(3,3,3)$ is a Draw. Any board $(m,n,3)$ with $\min(m,n) \ge 3$ and $\max(m,n) \ge 4$ is a First-Player Win. Any board with $\min(m,n) \le 2$ is a Draw.
  • k = 4 (Q-Gomoku): Patashnik (1980) and Uiterwijk (2002) resolved $(5,5,4)$ as a Draw. $(6,5,4)$ is a Win. $(4,n,4)$ is a Draw for $n \le 8$ and a Win for $n \ge 9$.
  • k = 5 (Standard Gomoku): In 1993, L. Victor Allis solved Freestyle Gomoku $(15,15,5)$ as a First-Player Win using Proof-Number Search (PNS). In 2020, Wei-Yuan Hsu and Chu-Ling Ko proved that both $(7,7,5)$ and $(8,8,5)$ are Draws. By monotonicity, $(m,n,5)$ is a Draw for all $m \le 8, n \le 8$. $(9,9,5)$ is a Win. Strip Gomoku of width 5 on infinite length remains an open research question.
  • k = 6 & k = 7 (The Open Frontier): $(7,7,6)$ and $(9,6,6)$ are proven draws via pairing strategies. However, whether the second player can force a draw on an infinite board $(\infty, \infty, 6)$ or $(\infty, \infty, 7)$ remains one of the premier open problems in combinatorial game theory. Both are conjectured to be draws.
  • k ≥ 8: Completely solved as Draws on all finite and infinite boards via Hales-Jewett pairing strategies and Ramsey bounds.
